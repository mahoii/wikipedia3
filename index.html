History

When recorded music first became popular, record labels controlled everything, recording, marketing, and distribution. Artists often signed one-sided contracts that gave labels ownership of their music. Musicians earned only a small percentage of sales (often just a few cents per record) while labels kept most of the profits. The unfair treatment of artists in the music industry dates back to the early 1900s, when record labels and managers began profiting far more than the musicians themselves. During the early days of jazz, blues, and rock ‘n’ roll, many Black artists were especially exploited. They were paid little or nothing while record companies made huge profits from their songs. In the 1950s and 60s, artists often signed unfair contracts that gave labels ownership of their music rights, leaving musicians with almost no control over their own work.

Significance

In 2021, Sir Lucian Grainge, CEO of Universal Music Group (UMG), was set to earn more than all UK songwriters combined made from music sales and streaming in 2019, largely due to massive bonuses after UMG’s stock market success. YouTube has warned that proposed UK laws forcing it to pay more for streamed music could harm artists instead of helping them. The company argued that stricter licensing rules could lead to “over-blocking” of content, which would reduce artists’ income. The music industry, however, accuses YouTube of exploiting a “value gap” — earning huge ad revenues while paying very little in royalties. In 2019, UK record labels made just £35 million from YouTube, compared to £66 million from vinyl sales. These pieces of information prove that companies, CEO’s and even streaming services care about profit and how much money goes into their pockets instead of these artists. The unfairness and imbalance in how money is distributed in the music industry, especially on how little artists and songwriters earn compared to big companies and executives.
