Overview

This blog presents an informative overview of unfair business practices in the music industry, the exploitation of artists, and how it has developed over time with the creation of streaming services like Spotify.

History

When recorded music first became popular, record labels controlled everything, recording, marketing, and distribution. Artists often signed one-sided contracts that gave labels ownership of their music. Musicians earned only a small percentage of sales (often just a few cents per record) while labels kept most of the profits. The practices in the music industry date back to the early 1900s, when record labels and managers began profiting far more than the musicians themselves. During the early days of jazz, blues, and rock ‘n’ roll, many artists (especially Black artists) were exploited. Since then, these types of exploitative practices have only doubled down and worsened, resulting in the overall monopolization of artists that we see today. With the creation and remarkable rise of streaming services like Spotify and Apple Music in the 2000s and 2010s, music consumption was transformed and destined to become the dominant method of music distribution and discourse. With this increase in accessibility, music should have become more equitable; allowing artists to reach wider audiences and earn fair compensation for their music as independent publishers. However, the reality has become far more complex.

Significance

Daniel Ek, CEO of Spotify, is currently worth 10 billion dollars, more than any musician in human history, off of simply publishing music. Not creating. Publishing. In 2021, Sir Lucian Grainge, CEO of Universal Music Group (UMG), was set to earn more than all UK songwriters combined made from music sales and streaming in 2019, largely due to massive bonuses after UMG’s stock market success. YouTube has warned that proposed UK laws forcing it to pay more for streamed music could harm artists instead of helping them. The company argued that stricter licensing rules could lead to “over-blocking” of content, which would reduce artists’ income. The music industry, however, accuses YouTube of exploiting a “value gap” — earning huge ad revenues while paying very little in royalties. In 2019, UK record labels made just £35 million from YouTube, compared to £66 million from vinyl sales. These pieces of information prove that companies, CEO’s and even streaming services care about profit and how much money goes into their pockets instead of these artists. The unfairness and imbalance in how money is distributed in the music industry, especially on how little artists and songwriters earn compared to big companies and executives.
